## Summary

Will Howell discusses launching Johns Hopkins’ School of Government and Policy in Washington, DC, and developing a graduate program beyond the traditional division between public policy analysis and public administration. That model assumed a stable Congress would set policy and a capable bureaucracy would implement it; he argues that polarization, administrative disruption, and declining trust in expertise challenge those assumptions. The school is consulting practitioners as it develops training in organizational systems, project management, technology, and institutional reform, aiming at effective public outcomes.

The conversation examines evidence use, government’s limited capacity to learn, and university incentives, including tenure criteria that privilege disciplinary publications over teaching or practical impact. Howell wants academics and practitioners to collaborate and sees history and technical knowledge of AI as relevant to institutional reform and governance. He describes commitments to constitutional government and pluralism, the risks of mistaking ambition for impact, and success as graduates taking up institutional reform—an effect likely to require years to assess.
